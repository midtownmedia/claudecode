We are co-writing a short story called "Strawberry Moon". I supply the plot beats; you write the prose. Two parts are drafted below. Please read the notes and the full draft, then wait for my next set of beats or edit requests. Do not rewrite what exists unless I ask.

## Story notes

**Premise.** A veterinarian in the Appalachian mountains hears strange sounds while closing up at night. They get louder and stay longer, and the building's electrics start failing around them. He is being stalked by an injured werewolf, who needs antibiotics. The werewolf, in wolf form, bites him. He becomes one.

**Setting.** The fictional town of Strawberry, in the Appalachian mountains. June. The June full moon is the Strawberry Moon. The clinic is the Strawberry Animal Clinic, at the dead end of Laurel Fork Road.

**Characters.**

- **Dr. Wade Hollis**: the vet. Nineteen years in practice. Tired, dry, notoriously cheap about repairs.
- **Dana**: head tech. Sharp-tongued. Drives a Subaru with a bad belt. Has been telling him to fix the wiring since the ice storm.
- **Cole**: tech. Jokes along with Dana.
- **The drifter**: the werewolf. Thin, bearded, army coat, yellow eyes. Shot by poachers. Calm and faintly amused in human form; "tends to overreact" as a wolf. Unnamed so far.
- **The deputy**: uninterested in stolen "dog pills".
- **Mrs. Earlene Tolliver**: client. Owns an elderly golden retriever and a prize flock of Australorp hens (Best of Breed at the county fair three years running). Has a two o'clock appointment on Friday.

**Rules established so far.**

- Electrics fail near a werewolf: computers restart, lights dim, flashlights die. After he is bitten, this happens around Wade himself.
- Animals react to him with terror or aggression after the bite.
- Bites and wounds on him heal within minutes.
- He blacks out during the change. The drifter says he and the wolf "will become one eventually" and will share memories after a few more moons.
- The drifter's advice: stay away from silver and poachers; stick to deer to avoid attention.
- There is no cure. The drifter laughed at the question.

**Voice.** Third person, close on Wade, past tense. Plain, concrete sentences. Dry humour that comes from the characters, never from the narrator winking. Horror played straight. Veterinary detail used where it earns its place.

**Length so far.** Part One is about 2,300 words; Part Two about 1,100.

**Open threads.**

- The dead prize hen: Wade has to lie to Earlene Tolliver on Friday.
- The handcuff still locked on his left wrist, chain snapped.
- What he tells Dana that morning.
- The poachers, and the distant rifle shot.
- Whose blood he woke up in after the first night.
- The drifter has no name and no history yet.
- The wolf's memories merging with his over the coming moons.

**Decision still open.** Part One ends with Wade waiting in the dark clinic office. Part Two opens with him losing his nerve and driving home. If I prefer, Part One's ending can be rewritten so he heads home directly.

## Full draft

# Strawberry Moon

## Part One

The first night, Dr. Wade Hollis blamed the compressor.

The Strawberry Animal Clinic sat at the dead end of Laurel Fork Road, where the pavement gave up and the mountain took over. The building had a walk-in cooler older than his techs and a roof that ticked as it cooled. So when something dragged itself along the back wall while he was counting the till, slow and heavy, he said "compressor" out loud to the empty lobby and went home.

The second night it was at the kennel door. It didn't scratch. It leaned, and the steel creaked in its frame the way a gate does when a cow puts her weight on it. The three boarders in the back, who barked at delivery trucks, at thunder, and at each other, made no sound at all. When he looked in on them they were pressed into the far corners of their runs, watching the door.

By the end of that week the sounds were arriving earlier and staying later. There was breathing under the exam room window, wet and labored. He knew that sound. He'd listened to it through a stethoscope for nineteen years, and it meant something was hurt.

Then the building started acting up. The front desk computer shut itself off and restarted while he was mid-invoice, twice, then four times. The lights in the surgery suite went out with the switch still up. He took the big Maglite from under the counter, and it died in his hand the moment he pointed it down the back hallway. In the lobby it came back on. He walked toward the hallway and it died again. It was like something back there was drinking the current.

"Stay late with me Thursday," he told Dana, his head tech. "Just an hour."

Dana looked at Cole. Cole looked at Dana.

"Is this like the time you put the rubber snake in the autoclave?" she asked.

"That was four years ago."

"I screamed in front of a client, Doc."

"There's something coming around at night. I need somebody else to hear it."

"Uh-huh." Cole was already grinning. "And when we're standing back there in the dark, what jumps out? You got a bear costume?"

They stayed Thursday anyway, giggling with their phones out to film whatever he had planned. The building stayed silent. Nothing dragged or breathed, and the lights held steady. At 7:15 Dana patted his shoulder, said, "Good effort, though," and they left him standing in the lobby.

At 7:40 the computer restarted.

---

Monday morning the pharmacy cabinet was open.

It hadn't been pried. The door hung from one hinge, and the metal around the lock was folded outward like a pulled tab. The cephalexin was gone, and the amoxicillin, the doxycycline, and every bottle of enrofloxacin he had. The shelves were swept clean and the boxes were shredded. On the linoleum there was a smear of blood the length of his arm. Stuck in it were tufts of coarse gray fur, longer than anything he'd ever pulled from a clipper blade.

The controlled-substance lockbox sat eighteen inches away, untouched.

The deputy who came out took one photo and didn't write anything down.

"Any ketamine missing? Tramadol? Anything of the opiate variety?"

"No. They left all that. That's what's strange about it. Whoever did this knew exactly what they—"

"So, dog pills."

"Antibiotics. About eleven hundred dollars of them."

"Dog pills," the deputy said again, and closed his notebook. "Probably a coon got in. I'd fix that door." He was back in his cruiser before Wade could ask what kind of raccoon folds steel.

Wade mopped up the blood himself. He bagged a tuft of the fur first, though he couldn't have said why.

---

It was Tuesday when the man came out of the tree line.

Wade had locked up at dusk, with the lightning bugs starting in the ditch. He was halfway to his truck when a voice behind him said, "Doctor."

The man was rail-thin and bearded and wore an army coat in June. He held his left arm tight against his ribs. Wade took him for a drifter. They came through sometimes, walking the rail bed.

"I need help," the man said.

Wade was tired, and he'd spent a week being laughed at. He said, "Unless you walk on four legs, buddy, you're in the wrong place."

The man lifted his head.

His eyes were yellow, the yellow of a struck match, and they held the last of the daylight and gave it back.

Wade's keys were in his hand. He turned, got the truck door open six inches, and a palm slammed it shut hard enough to rock the truck on its springs. The man had been twenty feet away. Now he was close enough that Wade could smell him. He smelled of infection, sweet and rotten, with creek mud under it and something like wet dog.

"I asked nice," the man said.

His eyes were brighter now. The hand on the truck door was changing while Wade watched. The fingers thickened and the knuckles swelled. The nails pushed out long and dark, and they ticked against the glass. A sound was coming from the man's chest. Wade had heard it from cornered shepherds and half-sedated mastiffs, but never from anything that stood upright. He felt it in his own sternum.

Nineteen years of practice had taught him one rule above the others. When an animal growls, you believe it.

"Okay," Wade said. "Okay. Show me."

The man pulled the coat aside. The wound sat below the ribs, ragged and black at the edges and weeping. It was a gunshot wound, at least a week old. The stolen pills hadn't touched it.

"How'd you get that?"

"Poachers."

Wade actually laughed, one short, cracked note. "For real?"

A rifle shot rolled down off the ridge, distant and flat.

The man flinched like he'd been hit again. The yellow went out of his eyes and left plain animal terror. Then he was running, bent low and faster than a hurt man should be able to move. The laurel closed behind him without a sound.

Wade stood in the lot until full dark. Then he drove home with the doors locked and told no one, because there wasn't anyone to tell.

---

Wednesday, after close, there was a dog by the dumpster.

It was a husky mix, or so he told himself. It was big and gray and rough-coated, and it lay on its side in the gravel with its ribs heaving. It had no collar. When he crouched it didn't raise its head, only rolled one eye toward him.

The eye was yellow. He decided not to think about that.

"Easy," he said. "Easy, big man."

He should have gone for the catch pole and the muzzle. Instead he half-carried it inside, ninety pounds at least, and got it on the table. He parted the matted fur below its ribs. The wound underneath was ragged and black at the edges.

His hands kept working while his mind refused to. He clipped and flushed and debrided. The animal shook but held still, right up until the chlorhexidine hit the deep tissue.

He never saw it move. One second he was irrigating. The next his left forearm was in its mouth, and he felt the teeth meet. It let go at once, almost apologetically, and lay there panting with his blood on its muzzle.

"Son of a *bitch*." He wrapped a towel around his arm. He was a professional, and the syringe was already drawn, so he gave the long-acting antibiotic one-handed, under the skin between the shoulders. "There. Two weeks' worth. You're welcome."

The dog came off the table, hit the back door hard enough to spring the latch, and was gone up the mountain.

He cleaned his arm at the scrub sink. There were four deep punctures and a tear. It was a bite he'd have sent a tech to urgent care for. But by the time he finished rinsing, the bleeding had stopped. By the time he found the gauze, the edges of the punctures had gone pink and tight, as though they were days old.

*Odd*, he thought, and taped it up, and went home.

---

The fever came on around ten. By eleven he was shaking so hard the bed frame knocked the wall. His teeth ached and his skin felt too small for him. He remembered getting up for water. He remembered the glass breaking in the sink, and the kitchen window, and how bright the moon was through it, nearly full.

After that he remembered nothing.

---

He woke up cold, face-down in leaves, with birds going overhead.

He was naked and plastered with mud and pine needles. He was covered in blood from chin to belly. It had dried, and it cracked when he moved. He checked himself with shaking hands. None of it was his. His forearm, where the bite had been, showed four pale dimples and nothing else.

He climbed until he could see something. It was the fire tower on Sorrel Knob. That was on the wrong side of the county, twenty miles from his bed, maybe more.

He went home like a criminal. He kept to creek beds and fence rows and crouched in the weeds when a school bus passed. He took a pair of work pants off a clothesline behind a farmhouse in Pickett's Gap and a flannel shirt off another one a mile on. A beagle on a chain watched him do it and never made a sound. It flattened itself in the dirt and wet the ground.

He stood in the shower while the water ran red, then pink, then clear. He sat on the edge of the tub with his head in his hands. He thought about encephalitis and rabies, and about psychotic breaks.

Then every alarm in the house went off at once, the phone and the clock and the backup. He was forty minutes late for work.

---

The barking started before he reached the door.

Every animal in the building was at it. The boarders howled. A cat in a carrier threw itself against the grate. Mrs. Tolliver's elderly golden had licked his face twice a year for a decade, and now it backed under a lobby chair and showed him its teeth.

"What did you *do*?" Dana shouted over the noise.

He was bitten four times before lunch. A dachshund got him first, then a border collie, then a cat he'd bottle-raised. The fourth was a Lab puppy, twelve weeks old, which latched onto his thumb and screamed while it did it. It was more bites in one day than he'd taken in his whole career. Each of them closed up before he could find a bandage, and he kept his hands in his pockets so nobody would see.

The lights were the other thing.

He noticed it first in Exam Two. He stepped through the door and the fluorescents dipped and buzzed and came back, like the building had flinched. He stepped out and they steadied. In the pharmacy the under-cabinet strip flickered until he left. In the treatment room the monitor on the dental cart rolled its picture, and the clippers in Cole's hand stuttered and then caught.

"Seriously?" Cole said to the ceiling.

Wade went to the break room for coffee. The microwave clock reset itself to 12:00 as he passed. He stood very still and watched it blink.

By noon the staff had worked it out to their own satisfaction.

"It's the wiring," Dana said. She said it from the front desk, to the whole lobby, loud enough to carry. "I've been telling him since the ice storm. Two years ago, Doc. I gave you the electrician's card. I put it in your hand."

"It's on my list."

"His list." She turned to Cole. "You know what else is on his list? The cooler. The roof. The back door that a *raccoon* apparently broke into."

"He re-uses the exam gloves," Cole offered.

"I do not re-use the exam gloves."

"You'd like to."

Wade walked back toward his office. The hallway lights dimmed ahead of him, one panel at a time, and came back up behind him as he passed. Neither of them saw the pattern. They only saw an old building and a cheap boss, and Dana was already hunting through the desk drawer for the electrician's card to put in his hand a second time.

He had stood in that hallway a week ago with a dead flashlight. He had thought then that something in the dark was drinking the current.

At two he shut himself in his office and typed with one finger, feeling like a fool. *Werewolf bite symptoms.* He got movie trivia, a forum, and a quiz. He read the page on clinical lycanthropy three times, because it was the only one that came with a prescription. *It's a delusion,* he told himself. *It's fever and stress and a dog bite. I sleepwalked.*

He had sleepwalked twenty miles. He had woken up in somebody else's blood.

He went to close the browser and his eye caught the corner of the feed store calendar on the wall. It was a free calendar with a tractor photo for every month. Today's square had a small open circle and four words in red.

*Full Moon — Strawberry Moon.*

So the full moon was tonight. Last night had only been the night before.

He was still staring at the calendar when Dana knocked to say they were heading out. Everything was fed and locked, and was he okay, because he didn't look okay. He heard himself say he was fine. He heard their cars pull out. Through the blinds he watched the light go amber on the ridge, then rose, then gray.

In the kennels, all the dogs went silent together.

On the desk in front of him, the computer screen went black and restarted.

Dr. Wade Hollis took the Maglite from the drawer and thumbed the switch. Nothing happened. He sat in the dark with it and waited for the moon.

## Part Two

He lasted four minutes in the dark office. Then he thought about Dana unlocking the front door in the morning and finding whatever would be left of the kennels, and he ran for the truck.

He made one stop, at the pawn shop on Main, ten minutes before it closed. The handcuffs were in the glass case between a deer rifle and a banjo. The man at the counter rang them up without a word. Wade was grateful for that, and a little troubled by it.

At home he pulled the blinds. He moved the kitchen knives to the top of the refrigerator, as if that would matter. He dragged the mattress off the bed for no reason he could have defended. The old cast-iron radiator in the front room was bolted through the floor into a joist. He knelt beside it and closed one cuff around his left wrist. The ratchet clicked. It sounded very small. He was holding the other cuff open over the radiator pipe and doing the math on the key when someone knocked on the door.

There were three knocks, and they were polite.

He opened it with the handcuff dangling from his wrist.

The drifter stood on the porch in the army coat with his hat in his hands. He stood straighter than he had on Tuesday. His color was better. He looked at the cuff, then at Wade, and seemed embarrassed.

"I came to say I'm sorry," he said. "About the arm. I tend to overreact in that form."

Wade opened his mouth, but he had nothing to say.

"The shot's working, though. So thank you." The man nodded at the handcuffs. "Those won't do anything. But I appreciate the thought. Most don't think of it."

"I don't—" Wade started. "What did you—"

The porch light was off, and the man's eyes were yellow anyway. The color came up slowly, like a lamp on a dimmer. Behind his shoulder the ridge had a white edge on it where the moon was about to come over.

Then the fever hit him like a train.

There was no build to it this time. He was on the floor before he knew he'd fallen. His back bowed. His jaw felt like it was being pulled out by the roots. He heard the coffee table go, and the bookshelf, and understood dimly that he was the one doing it. The cuff bit into his wrist and then stopped biting. He put his hand through the drywall and it came back wrong, too long, with too many angles in it. He screamed and it didn't sound like him.

Across the wrecked room the drifter had stepped inside and closed the door behind him. He hadn't moved since. He stood with his arms loose at his sides and let it come. His shoulders rose and his spine lengthened. His face drew forward slowly and without any hurry, like a man stretching after a long drive. He did not thrash and he did not make a sound. As the muzzle pushed out and the lips pulled back along it, Wade saw that he was smiling. The smile got longer and longer.

Then there was nothing.

---

He woke to gravel against his cheek and a mourning dove calling.

He knew the gravel. He lifted his head and saw his own sign, STRAWBERRY ANIMAL CLINIC, with a paw print for the apostrophe that had never made grammatical sense. He was in the parking lot. He was naked. The sky was the gray of about half past six.

He was holding a dead chicken by the neck.

He looked at it for a long moment. It was a large hen, glossy black, and it had clearly been a healthy bird. He set it down on the gravel very gently, as though that would help.

The handcuff was still locked on his left wrist. Three links of chain hung from it. The last one was pulled open like taffy.

The spare key was under the fake rock, where it had been for nine years. He was inside and into a set of scrubs in ninety seconds. Every dog in the kennel screamed at him the whole time. He scrubbed his face and forearms at the surgery sink until the water stopped running pink. He pulled a long-sleeved jacket over the cuff. Then he remembered the chicken.

He ran back outside. The drifter was crouched in the lot, picking it up.

He was in the army coat again, barefoot, with leaves in his beard. He turned the bird over in his hands like a man checking produce.

"Is this real?" Wade's voice came out hoarse and shredded. "Is this actually happening? Am I a werewolf now?"

The drifter smiled the same patient smile and nodded.

"Stay away from silver," he said. "And poachers. Stick to deer to avoid raising attention." He held the hen up by its feet. A numbered aluminum band shone on one leg, the kind they put on show birds. "This chicken here is going to cause you some headaches. I hope you're a good liar."

Wade stared at the band. He knew that band. He'd vaccinated that flock. Those were Earlene Tolliver's Australorps, three years running Best of Breed at the county fair. Earlene had a two o'clock appointment on Friday.

"I'm gonna black out every full moon?"

"For a while." The drifter tucked the hen under his arm. "You and the wolf will become one eventually. You'll share memories after a few more moons. It's easier then. You'll remember where you left your clothes, for one thing."

"Is there a cure?"

The drifter laughed. It wasn't unkind. It was the laugh of a man who'd been asked whether the mountain could be moved a little to the left. He was still laughing as he crossed the lot. He stepped over the ditch and went up into the laurel with the chicken swinging from his hand. The woods closed behind him.

Wade stood in the gravel in his scrubs. The sun came up over Sorrel Knob. Down the road he could hear Dana's Subaru with its bad belt, a quarter mile off and getting closer.

He pulled his sleeve down over the handcuff and started working out what he was going to tell her.
